Recall the definition of a Fourier transform.

\begin{definition}\label{def:Fourier-Transform}\lean{FourierTransform.fourier}\leanok
  The Fourier transform of an $L^1$-function $f:\R^d\to\C$ is defined as

  \[
    \mathcal{F}(f)(y) = \widehat{f}(y) := \int_{\R^d} f(x)e^{-2\pi i \langle x, y \rangle} \,\mathrm{d}x, \quad y \in \R^d
  \]

  where $\langle x, y \rangle = \frac12\|x\|^2 + \frac12\|y\|^2 - \frac12\|x - y\|^2$ is the standard scalar product in $\R^d$.
\end{definition}

The following computational result will be of use later on.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:Gaussian-Fourier}\uses{def:Fourier-Transform}\lean{fourier_gaussian_innerProductSpace}
  For every $z \in \h$, the Gaussian $x \mapsto e^{\pi i \|x\|^2 z}$ on $\R^8$ satisfies
  \begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}(e^{\pi i \|x\|^2 z})(y) = z^{-4}\,e^{\pi i \|y\|^2 \,(\frac{-1}{z}) }.
  \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Mathlib knows the Fourier transform of the Gaussian $x \mapsto e^{-b\|x\|^2}$ on a $d$-dimensional inner product space for any $b \in \C$ with $\Re(b) > 0$: it equals $y \mapsto (\pi/b)^{d/2}\,e^{-\pi^2\|y\|^2/b}$ (see \verb|fourier_gaussian_innerProductSpace|). Taking $d = 8$ and $b = -\pi i z$, which satisfies $\Re(b) = \pi \Im(z) > 0$, we obtain
  \[
    \left(\frac{\pi}{-\pi i z}\right)^{4} e^{-\pi^2\|y\|^2/(-\pi i z)}
    = z^{-4}\, e^{\pi i \|y\|^2(-1/z)},
  \]
  where no branch-cut issues arise because the exponent $d/2 = 4$ is an integer.
\end{proof}

Of great interest to us will be a specific family of functions, known as Schwartz Functions. The Fourier transform behaves particularly well when acting on Schwartz functions. We elaborate in the following subsections.

\subsection{On Schwartz Functions}

\begin{definition}\label{def:Schwartz-Space}\lean{SchwartzMap}\leanok
A $C^\infty$~function $f:\R^d\to\C$ is called a \emph{Schwartz function} if it decays to zero as $\|x\|\to\infty$ faster then any inverse power of $\|x\|$, and the same holds for all partial derivatives of $f$, ie, if for all $k \in \N$ and $n \in \N^d$, there exists a constant $C \in \R$ such that for all $x \in \R^d$, $\norm{x}^k \cdot \abs{f^{(n)}(x)} \leq C$, where $f^{(n)}$ denotes the $n$-th partial derivative of $f$. The set of all Schwartz functions from $\R^d$ to $\C$ is called the \emph{Schwartz space}. It is an $\R$-vector space. (The Mathlib definition \verb|SchwartzMap| is stated, more generally, for functions between normed spaces $E \to F$, with the $n$-th iterated Fr\'echet derivative and its operator norm in place of partial derivatives; the two formulations agree for $\R^d \to \C$. The same generality applies to the results below.)
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:Fourier-transform-is-automorphism}\lean{SchwartzMap.fourierTransformCLM}\uses{def:Fourier-Transform, def:Schwartz-Space}\leanok
  The Fourier transform is a continuous, linear automorphism of the space of Schwartz functions.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}\leanok
  We do not elaborate here as the result already exists in Mathlib. We do, however, mention that the Lean implementation \emph{defines} a continuous linear equivalence on the Schwartz space \emph{using} the Fourier transform (see \verb|SchwartzMap.fourierTransformCLM|). The `proof' that for any Schwartz function $f$, its Fourier transform and its image under this continuous linear equivalence are, indeed, the same $\R^d \to \R$ function, is stated in Mathlib solely for the purpose of \verb|rw| and \verb|simp| tactics, and is proven simply by \verb|rfl|.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Radial Functions and Fourier Eigenfunctions}

The functions we construct in Section~\ref{sec: fourier double zeroes} will be \emph{radial}, and we record here the elementary facts about radial Schwartz functions that drive that construction.

\begin{definition}\label{def:radial-function}
  Let $E$ be a normed $\R$-vector space. A function $f : E \to \C$ is \emph{radial} if $f(x) = f(y)$ whenever $\norm{x} = \norm{y}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:fourier-involution}\uses{def:Schwartz-Space, lemma:Fourier-transform-is-automorphism}\lean{MagicFunction.a.Fourier.fourier_involution}\leanok
  For every Schwartz function $f : \R^d \to \C$ and every $x \in \R^d$,
  \[
    \mathcal{F}(\mathcal{F}(f))(x) = f(-x).
  \]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}\leanok
  Schwartz functions are integrable with integrable Fourier transforms, so the Fourier inversion theorem (available in Mathlib as \verb|Continuous.fourierInv_fourier_eq|, together with the identity $\mathcal{F}^{-1}(f)(x) = \mathcal{F}(f)(-x)$) applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:radial-fourier-inversion}\uses{def:radial-function, lemma:fourier-involution}\lean{MagicFunction.a.Fourier.radial_inversion}\leanok
  If $f : \R^d \to \C$ is an even Schwartz function --- in particular, if $f$ is radial --- then $\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{F}(f)) = f$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}\leanok
  Immediate from Lemma~\ref{lemma:fourier-involution}, since $f(-x) = f(x)$.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lemma:radial-fourier-inversion} says that $\mathcal{F}$ is an involution on radial Schwartz functions, so such functions decompose into eigenfunctions of $\mathcal{F}$ with eigenvalues $\pm 1$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:eigenfunction-decomposition}\uses{lemma:radial-fourier-inversion}
  Let $f : \R^d \to \C$ be a radial Schwartz function. Then there exist unique functions $f_+$ and $f_-$ with $f = f_+ + f_-$, $\mathcal{F}(f_+) = f_+$ and $\mathcal{F}(f_-) = -f_-$, namely $f_\pm = \frac12 (f \pm \widehat{f})$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Setting $f_\pm := \frac12(f \pm \widehat{f})$, Lemma~\ref{lemma:radial-fourier-inversion} gives $\mathcal{F}(f_\pm) = \frac12(\widehat{f} \pm f) = \pm f_\pm$. Conversely, if $f = f_+ + f_-$ with $\mathcal{F}(f_\pm) = \pm f_\pm$, then $\widehat{f} = f_+ - f_-$, and solving the two equations recovers $f_\pm = \frac12(f \pm \widehat{f})$.
\end{proof}

This decomposition reduces the construction of the magic function in Section~\ref{sec: g} to the construction of one $+1$- and one $-1$-eigenfunction of $\mathcal{F}$, which is carried out in Section~\ref{sec: fourier double zeroes}.

The decaying property of Schwartz functions means that they can be compared to the absolutely convergent power series above.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:Schwartz-summable}\uses{def:Schwartz-Space}
  Let $f : \R^d \to \C$ be a Schwartz function and let $X \subset \R^d$ be periodic with respect to some lattice $\Lambda \subset \R^d$. Then,
  \[
    \sum_{x \in X} |f(x)| < \infty.
  \]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Without loss of generality, assume that $0 \notin X$: if $0 \in X$, then we can add the $f(0)$ term to the sum over nonzero elements of $X$, which, if the sum over the nonzero elements converges absolutely, will be equal to the sum over all of $X$. Now, we know that for all $k \in \N$, there exists some constant $C$ such that $|f(x)| \leq C\norm{x}^{-k}$ for all $x \in \R^d$. Choosing $k$ to be sufficiently large, we see that
  \[
    \sum_{x \in X} |f(x)| \leq \sum_{x \in X} \frac{C}{\norm{x}^{k}} = C \sum_{x \in X} \frac{1}{\norm{x}^k} < \infty.
  \]
\end{proof}

We end with a crucial result on Schwartz functions, the statement of which only makes sense because of the above result.

\begin{theorem}[Poisson summation formula]\label{thm:Poisson-summation-formula}\uses{def:Fourier-Transform, def:Schwartz-Space, lemma:Schwartz-summable, lemma:inv-power-summable, def:dual-lattice}\lean{SchwartzMap.PoissonSummation_Lattices}\leanok
  Let $\Lambda$ be a lattice in $\R^d$, and let $f:\R^d\to\R$ be a Schwartz function. Then, for all $v \in \R^d$,
  \[
    \sum_{\ell\in\Lambda}f(\ell + v) = \frac{1}{\Vol{\R^d/\Lambda}} \sum_{m\in\Lambda^*}\widehat{f}(m) e^{-2\pi i \ang{v, m}}.
  \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  One possible proof would be by induction on $d$. However, there are numerous nuances involved, particularly in manipulating nested infinite sums. Ideas would be appreciated.
\end{proof}

While the Poisson Summation Formula over lattices can be stated in greater generality (and probably should be formalised as such in Mathlib due to the many applications it admits), we stick with Schwartz functions because that should be sufficient for our purposes.

\subsection{Radial Schwartz Functions from One-Dimensional Data}\label{subsec:radial-schwartz-bridge}

Later, we will use Theorem \ref{thm:Poisson-summation-formula} to prove that certain functions $a(x)$ and $b(x)$, defined in Section~\ref{sec: fourier double zeroes}, are eigenfunctions of the Fourier transform. To apply the theorem, we need to show that these functions are Schwartz. Both are of the form $x \mapsto f(\|x\|^2)$ for a function $f : \R \to \C$ given by contour integrals, so we want a criterion for Schwartzness of such compositions purely in terms of $f$. This subsection provides one.

The first ingredient is a composition theorem already in Mathlib.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:schwartz-comp-normsq}\uses{def:Schwartz-Space, def:radial-function}\lean{schwartzMap_multidimensional_of_schwartzMap_real}\leanok
  Let $f \in \mathcal{S}(\R, \C)$ be a Schwartz function. Then, for every $d \in \N$, the radial function
  \[
    f_d : \R^d \to \C, \quad f_d(x) := f(\|x\|^2)
  \]
  is a Schwartz function.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}\leanok
  Mathlib's \verb|SchwartzMap.compCLM| (due to M.~Doll) shows that precomposition with a function of temperate growth that grows at least polynomially produces a (continuous linear) map between Schwartz spaces. The function $x \mapsto \|x\|^2$ has temperate growth (\verb|Function.hasTemperateGrowth_norm_sq|) and satisfies the required lower bound $\|x\| \le 1 + \|x\|^2$, so the composition $f \circ \|\cdot\|^2$ is Schwartz.
\end{proof}

Lemma~\ref{lemma:schwartz-comp-normsq} is not directly applicable to the functions of Section~\ref{sec: fourier double zeroes}: the one-dimensional integrals defining them are \emph{not} Schwartz on all of $\R$ --- they grow exponentially as $r \to -\infty$, and some of them are not even given by convergent integrals far to the left of the origin. However, only their behaviour on $[0, \infty)$ matters, since $\|x\|^2 \geq 0$. The remedy is to multiply by a smooth cutoff before composing.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:smooth-cutoff}\lean{Real.smoothTransition}
  There exists a smooth function $\Theta : \R \to [0, 1]$ such that $\Theta(x) = 0$ for $x \leq -1$ and $\Theta(x) = 1$ for $x \geq 0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Take $\Theta(x) := \theta(x + 1)$, where $\theta$ is Mathlib's smooth transition function \verb|Real.smoothTransition|, which is smooth, takes values in $[0,1]$, vanishes on $(-\infty, 0]$ and equals $1$ on $[1, \infty)$. Explicitly, $\theta(x) = \frac{\rho(x)}{\rho(x) + \rho(1-x)}$ where $\rho(x) = e^{-1/x}$ for $x > 0$ and $\rho(x) = 0$ for $x \leq 0$.
\end{proof}

Note that, being smooth and constant on both $(-\infty, -1]$ and $[0, \infty)$, the function $\Theta$ has all of its derivatives supported in the compact interval $[-1, 0]$; in particular $\Theta$ and all of its derivatives are (individually) bounded. Rescaling, for any $\theta < 0$ we obtain a smooth cutoff $\Theta_\theta(x) := \Theta\left(\frac{2x}{-\theta}\right)$ which vanishes for $x \leq \theta/2$, equals $1$ for $x \geq 0$, and transitions on $[\theta/2, 0]$; for $\theta = -2$ this is $\Theta$ itself.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cutoff-schwartz}\uses{def:Schwartz-Space, lemma:smooth-cutoff}
  Let $\theta < 0$ and let $f : \R \to \C$ be smooth on $(\theta, \infty)$ and such that for all $k, n \in \N$ there exists $C \in \R$ with
  \[
    x^{k} \cdot |f^{(n)}(x)| \leq C \quad \text{for all } x \geq 0.
  \]
  Then $\Theta_\theta \cdot f$ (extended by zero where $f$ is undefined) is a Schwartz function on $\R$; it agrees with $f$ on $[0, \infty)$ and vanishes on $(-\infty, \theta/2]$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}\uses{lemma:smooth-cutoff}
  Write $g := \Theta_\theta \cdot f$. The last two assertions are immediate from the corresponding properties of $\Theta_\theta$.

  \emph{Smoothness.} The open sets $(-\infty, \theta/2)$ and $(\theta, \infty)$ cover $\R$. On the former, $g$ vanishes identically; on the latter, $g$ is a product of smooth functions. Since smoothness is a local property, $g$ is smooth on $\R$.

  \emph{Decay.} Fix $k, n \in \N$. By the Leibniz rule,
  \[
    g^{(n)} = \sum_{j=0}^{n} \binom{n}{j}\, \Theta_\theta^{(j)}\, f^{(n-j)}.
  \]
  We bound $|x|^k\,|g^{(n)}(x)|$ on three regions. For $x \leq \theta/2$, $g^{(n)}(x) = 0$. For $x \geq 0$, the cutoff is constant, so $\Theta_\theta^{(j)}(x) = 0$ for all $j \geq 1$ and $g^{(n)}(x) = f^{(n)}(x)$, which satisfies the required bound by hypothesis. Finally, on the compact interval $[\theta/2, 0]$, the factor $|x|^k$ is bounded by $|\theta/2|^k$, each $\Theta_\theta^{(j)}$ is bounded, and each $f^{(n-j)}$ is continuous on the compact set $[\theta/2, 0] \subset (\theta, \infty)$, hence bounded. Taking the maximum of the three bounds completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:schwartz-of-nonneg-decay}\uses{thm:cutoff-schwartz, lemma:schwartz-comp-normsq}
  Let $\theta < 0$ and let $f : \R \to \C$ be as in Theorem~\ref{thm:cutoff-schwartz}. Then, for all $d \in \N$, the function
  \[
    f_d : \R^d \to \C, \quad f_d(x) := f(\|x\|^2)
  \]
  is a Schwartz function.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}\uses{thm:cutoff-schwartz, lemma:schwartz-comp-normsq}
  Since $\|x\|^2 \geq 0$ and $\Theta_\theta = 1$ on $[0, \infty)$, we have $f_d = (\Theta_\theta f) \circ \|\cdot\|^2$ as functions on $\R^d$. The inner function $\Theta_\theta f$ is Schwartz by Theorem~\ref{thm:cutoff-schwartz}, so Lemma~\ref{lemma:schwartz-comp-normsq} applies.
\end{proof}

In the formalisation, the Schwartz structures on $a$ and $b$ are built exactly this way: the one-dimensional integrals are multiplied by a smooth cutoff (with $\theta = -2$ for $a$ and $\theta = -1$ for $b$, matching the regions where the corresponding integrals converge; see Section~\ref{sec: fourier double zeroes}), and the resulting Schwartz functions on $\R$ are transported to $\R^8$ via Lemma~\ref{lemma:schwartz-comp-normsq}. The cutoff is invisible after composing with $\|\cdot\|^2$, so the resulting Schwartz functions are \emph{equal} to the radial functions defined in Section~\ref{sec: fourier double zeroes}.
